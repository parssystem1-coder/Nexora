## 2026-09-03 — ADR-054: per-tenant recovery, and the two things it deliberately leaves unowned

**Context:** `RISK_REGISTER.md` **R-038** has recorded since 2026-09-02 that the only recovery mechanism this platform has is PostgreSQL physical point-in-time recovery, which operates on the whole cluster — so recovering one tenant rolls every other tenant back with it. For a platform whose entire isolation model is per-tenant, recovery was the one place tenancy did not exist. Earlier the same day Phase 2.5 was given the **export** half (ADR-020 rule 6). The **restore** half — the maintainer's actual requirement, *"store #357 broke, put it back"* — had no decision and no phase. Documentation-only session: no migration, table, module, capability, job or infrastructure.

**Options considered:** the granularity ruling was the maintainer's and is an input, not a conclusion drawn here. Four judgement calls were this session's:

- **Whether to state an RTO.** No. ADR-010's numeric targets are already flagged by dated amendment as unverified assumptions until `06` Phase 4 item 9, and a recovery-time number nobody has measured is worse than none, because it is the kind of number that gets quoted to a customer. What is stated is the *shape* — restore time scales with one tenant's volume rather than the cluster's — with the number owed to the first drill.
- **Whether to open a risk row for total cluster loss.** No — ADR-054 recommends one and does not open it, because the scope of a new row is the maintainer's to set. Recorded as a recommendation rather than silently skipped.
- **Whether to re-rate R-025.** No, but its staleness is recorded. See below.
- **Whether to restate or rename Phase 2.5.** Restated its framing; did not rename or split it. See below.

**Decision:**

**ADR-054 — Per-Tenant Recovery from Nightly Snapshots, `ACCEPTED (new)`, ruled by the maintainer on 2026-09-03.**

**The granularity ruling and what it bought.** Recovery granularity is **the last nightly snapshot: an RPO of 24 hours**, with arbitrary per-tenant point-in-time recovery **explicitly declined on cost**. The ADR records *why that makes it cheap*, because that reasoning is the thing a later reader would otherwise have to re-derive before pricing a reopening: arbitrary per-tenant PITR would have required all three of **continuous WAL archiving** (retained, monitored, and itself able to stop working silently), **a standby restore target** kept warm enough to replay into, and **a full-cluster restore per incident** from which one tenant's rows are then extracted — meaning recovery time scales with the whole cluster even when one tenant is affected. A nightly per-tenant logical snapshot requires **none of the three**. That is the difference between a scheduled job and an operational discipline.

**Snapshot and export are one mechanism, and the ADR says so rather than leaving it implied.** A nightly snapshot and a tenant's own data export are the same extraction on different schedules with different consumers — one unattended and written where an operator can reach it, the other on a tenant's request under their own quota. It is stated explicitly because two mechanisms for one thing is what `AGENTS.md` §4 forbids and what the idempotency rule already had to prevent once, and because **the specific failure mode is silent**: two independently-written extractors drift, one quietly stops covering a table the other covers, and nobody finds out until a restore is attempted. They are therefore built together and remain **separate deliverables** — the export is a capability with a quota, the snapshot is a job, the restore is an operator action.

**The ledger asymmetry, as a design property rather than a limitation.** Mutable tenant-owned rows are restored from the snapshot; the eight ledger-shaped tables are **never rewound** — verified against `PHASE_2_BRIEF.md` §5's seven (`subscription_periods`, `subscription_state_transitions`, `usage_ledger_entries`, `billing_payment_events`, `invoices`, `invoice_lines`, `outbox_events`) plus `audit_events` from Phase 1's own `20260822100100_audit__enforce_append_only.sql`, rather than recited from the session brief. **An invoice issued after the snapshot point stays issued**; undoing it is a compensating entry, which is what a financial record demands regardless of this ADR. Rewinding a ledger would be the defect, not the feature. The consequence is structural: the application role *cannot* delete a ledger row by design, so **restore is not something the running system can perform on itself** — it runs outside the application under a different role, as an **operator action with its own audit trail (ADR-034), never a tenant-facing capability.**

**A snapshot that has never been restored is not a backup.** A periodic automated drill restores a tenant into a sandbox and **verifies the result**, not merely that the restore exited zero. It carries its own verification checkbox rather than a sentence of prose, because it is the item every backup design drops first and discovers missing at the worst moment.

**Retention is 30 days, and the alignment is the reason.** It tracks ADR-020 rule 3's reversible-deletion waiting period so the platform has one retention number rather than two that drift apart. **One precision the ADR keeps:** rule 3 says the waiting period *default* is 30 days — a default, not a constant — so if it moves, snapshot retention follows it. The verification list has its own checkbox for that.

**The two things this ADR deliberately leaves unowned, both stated so a reader cannot close it believing more was solved than was:**

- **Total cluster or server loss.** A different risk with a different mechanism — physical backup plus off-site replication — and per-tenant logical snapshots do **not** substitute for it, because snapshots stored on infrastructure that is itself lost are not a recovery path. It is covered nowhere in this repository. **ADR-054 recommends a risk row and does not open one.**
- **Object storage, and this one blocks Phase 2.5's ability to build any of the recovery work at all.** Snapshots must be written somewhere and this platform has no object storage. `PHASE_2_BRIEF.md` §4 records `files` as out of Phase 2 with *"object storage, no phase owns it yet"*, and **R-025** already tracks it, confirming directly that no port exists (`platform/` holds only `clock.ts`, `config.ts`, `db/`, `http/`, `rate-limit/`) and no phase list delivers one. It is recorded as a **named prerequisite of Phase 2.5** rather than as an assumption inside the mechanism.

**A consequence this session caused and did not leave unsaid: R-025's own ratings are now stale.** It reads *"Low near-term — two full phases away from current work"* and *"Low today, Medium by Phase 3"* — both accurate when written on 2026-08-28, neither accurate now that ADR-054 makes object storage a prerequisite of the **next** phase blocking three of its four recovery items. R-025 carries a dated addendum recording that, with the superseded text preserved. **Re-rating its Likelihood and Impact cells is left to the maintainer** rather than taken as a side effect: this session's remit was ADR-054 and R-038, and a re-rating on an inherited external-review row should be deliberate.

**One further edge ADR-054 names rather than assumes:** what a restore does to a restored tenant's **sessions**. ADR-053 gives sessions a 30-day retention and `sessions` has no `tenant_id` at all, so it is not tenant-partitionable the way the snapshot mechanism assumes. Whether a restored tenant's users are logged out is unanswered, and an implementer should read that sentence as confirmation no answer was given.

**R-038's status word: `OPEN`.** Quoted from the row: *"nothing was built: no snapshot exists, no job runs, and a tenant that breaks tonight still cannot be recovered without rolling back every other tenant — the register reserves `RESOLVED` for a risk that is eliminated, not one that has acquired a good design."* The row also records that **ADR-020 rule 7's tenant-facing documentation obligation is now materially closer to an owner**: it needed a retention window to document, and ADR-054 supplies two concrete numbers — a 24-hour RPO and 30-day snapshot retention — so what was an obligation with nothing to state is now an obligation with content, still owed to whichever tenant-facing document Phase 2.5 produces.

**Phase 2.5 amended** in `PHASE_2_BRIEF.md` §9.6–§9.8 and `06_IMPLEMENTATION_PLAN.md`, dated, in each file's own style — the precedent for dated amendments in both was established earlier the same day. Items 4–7 added: snapshot job, operator restore, drill, and object storage as the prerequisite of the first three. The amendment says items 4–6 **share a mechanism** with the export capability already in §9.3, which is a different statement from being the same item, and records that they have **no dependency on the discount and referral work in either direction** — their only hard dependency is object storage.

**On Phase 2.5's framing, reported rather than done silently.** The phase was created as *Commercial Growth* and now carries operational resilience too. §9.8 records the unifying reading — **Phase 2 makes the product sellable; Phase 2.5 makes it responsible to run for real customers**: you can price it flexibly and you can be trusted with the data. **The caveat is recorded with it**: that reading is coherent but not forced, since nothing prevents selling at list price, so "must exist before the first paying tenant" is truer of the recovery half than the discount half. **The name `Commercial Growth` now under-describes the phase, and whether to rename it or split it is explicitly left to the maintainer** — a phase split is a maintainer decision and this session did not take it.

**Every premise the session brief asserted was checked before writing, and all three flagged ones held**, with one refinement: ADR-020 rule 3 says the waiting period *default* is 30 days rather than a fixed 30, so the ADR states the alignment as tracking the default rather than hard-coding a number. The ledger list matched §5 plus `audit_events` exactly, and the object-storage exclusion is real and better-located than the brief suggested — already tracked as R-025 rather than only as a §4 exclusion line.

**Status:** **RESOLVED** — for what this session could reach. The restore decision is made, its phase is assigned, and its two unowned dependencies are named rather than absorbed. What remains owed: **object storage**, which blocks three of Phase 2.5's four recovery items and belongs to no phase; **a risk row for total cluster loss**, recommended and not opened; **a measured RTO**, owed to the first drill; **the sessions-on-restore question**; and R-025's re-rating, left to the maintainer.

## 2026-09-02 — a mechanical register-integrity check, R-001's rendered status repaired, and ADR-051 drafted for R-036

**Context:** three leftovers, none blocking Phase 2 slice 1, grouped because the first finds the second. `RISK_REGISTER.md` is one large Markdown table whose rows have broken its own rendering **three times, by three different mechanisms** — a row spanning multiple physical lines (`b629cf1`, 2026-08-27); the same defect reintroduced by a later edit to the same row; and unescaped `|` inside inline code spans creating phantom cells (found 2026-09-02, affecting R-008, R-034 and R-001). **Options considered:**

**Where the integrity check belongs — the question this session was told not to decide silently.** **ADR-030 does not admit it, and no amendment was needed, because a better home already exists.** ADR-030 is titled *Architecture Conformance Enforcement*; every check its §2 enumerates concerns source code or the database (dependency direction, forbidden imports, singletons, schema, secrets). The well-formedness of a governance document is neither. The tempting counter-argument — that the harness already carries `ERROR-CODE-UNDOCUMENTED`, which is outside §2's list and reads `05_API_CAPABILITY_CONTRACTS.md` — does not survive inspection: that rule's *subject* is still code, checked against a contract document. This one's subject is a document's own syntax.

The deciding evidence is the repository's own convention. **Three mechanical artifact-integrity checks already exist outside the harness** — `npm run graph -- --check`, `npm run openapi -- --check`, `npm run check:dist-deps` — each with its own `npm run` script, its own spec under `tools/`, and its own CI step beside the conformance step. A register-integrity check is precisely that shape. Building it there required **no change to an accepted ADR at all**, which is strictly more conservative than either amending ADR-030 or quietly widening it.

That choice also settles the suppression question the session raised. `tools/conformance/lib/exceptions.ts` applies `exceptions.json` **generically** to every harness violation, matching on `rule` + `file`, with no per-rule opt-out. Had this been a conformance rule, `{"rule": "REGISTER-ROW-CELLS", "file": "RISK_REGISTER.md", "adr": "ADR-030"}` would have silenced it. **Because it does not run inside the harness, no `exceptions.json` entry can reach it** — a property, not an accident, and there is no ADR-backed reason a register row should be malformed for an exception to justify.

**Commit order was inverted relative to the session's instruction, deliberately.** The instruction had the rule land first so it would fail on live drift before the repair. The *evidence* for that is captured below and is what matters; the *commit* order is a different question, and committing the rule first would have put a red `npm test` and a red CI step into the history, breaking bisect at that commit. The repair is committed first and the rule second, so **every commit is green and each is revertible alone** — and reverting the repair alone now correctly makes the check fail, which is the property worth having.

**Decision:**

**Item 1 — `npm run check:register`.** `tools/register/check-register.ts`, wired into CI beside `graph -- --check`. Three assertions, narrow on purpose per ADR-043's lesson that *a brittle parser that silently matches nothing would be a check that always passes*: `REGISTER-HEADER` (header and delimiter rows intact, and the table actually found), `REGISTER-ROW-CELLS` (exactly 8 rendered cells, counting `\|` as content), `REGISTER-ROW-LINES` (every row on exactly one physical line). It validates **no cell content** — not the status vocabulary, not date formats, not ID sequence — because those are judgement calls the preamble owns and a check that guessed would produce false positives on correct rows.

Six fixtures behind ten test cases in `tools/register/register-integrity.spec.ts`: one per real defect mechanism, **a clean one** (without which a check that rejects everything also looks like it works), and **a vacuity fixture** — a register whose header is intact but whose rows no longer match the detection pattern, which must fail loudly rather than pass on an empty result set. That last is enforced by a `MIN_ROWS = 10` floor, set far below the real count of 40 so it never needs bumping, and far above zero so a broken parser cannot slip through.

**One premise in the session's own description of the guard chain did not survive verification, and the ADR is written on the corrected one.** The third outcome is **`CONFLICT`** (`revoke-membership.service.ts:96`, *"This membership is already revoked."*), **not `CONCURRENCY_CONFLICT`**. The ADR records one dependency an implementer must confirm before building Option B: it needs *"a session existed and was revoked"* to be distinguishable from *"no such session"* at guard time, which is **ADR-029**'s territory and is not assumed here.

**Status:** **OPEN** — ADR-051 is drafted and unruled. ## 2026-09-02 — R-008's root cause found: there was never an unexplained mode, only a misread stack line

**Context:** `main` had been red on `apps/api/membership-revoke.integration.spec.ts`'s two-concurrent-owners test across four CI occurrences since 2026-08-26. `PHASE_1_DEBT_CLOSURE.md` D-6 had already spent **165 local reproduction attempts** (150 isolated-scenario loops, 15 full local suite runs) and returned zero anomalies, refuting the deadlock hypothesis outright (`pg_stat_database.deadlocks` delta of 0; no 5xx in any occurrence). Three investigations had left the root cause `UNDETERMINED`. The record described **two** failure modes: an explained guard-timing `403` (occurrence 4 only) and an unexplained one in which `successes` read empty **despite the server log showing a clean 200** for the test's own uniquely-identified organization — a contradiction the record stated openly and could not resolve.

That contradiction was the whole difficulty, and it was an artifact.

**Options considered:** the session's instruction proposed three lines of attack — an environmental differential between the local machine and CI, a hypothesis that the client lost a response the server really sent, and the `fileParallelism: false` + `pg_stat_activity` experiment R-008 had named and never run. A fourth was available and was tried first because it was cheapest: **re-read the primary evidence rather than the summary of it.** The archived CI logs were still within retention, and nobody had gone back to them since the record's own occurrence-4 addendum discovered that the AssertionError message is ambiguous between the test's two assertions.

**Decision:**

**Root cause: DETERMINED. All four occurrences are the same, already-explained mode. The "unexplained" one never existed.**

The archived logs for all four runs were retrieved (`gh run view <id> --log-failed`, and `--attempt 1` for occurrences 2 and 3, whose reruns had otherwise replaced the failing attempt) and the **Vitest stack frame** read instead of the message. Every frame points at `expect(losses)`; **not one points at `expect(successes)`**:

| Occurrence | Run | Commit | Frame | Line at that commit |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | `32987662296` | `09a8017` | `:452:20` | `expect(losses).toHaveLength(1);` |
| 2 | `33080832110` | `cfd13f3` | `:464:20` | `expect(losses).toHaveLength(1);` |

**So `successes` had length 1 in every occurrence — exactly one request returned 200, every single time — and `losses` was the empty one**, meaning the other request returned a status that is neither 409 nor 401. Occurrence 4's diagnostic dump names it: `statuses: [200, 403]`, `bodyBtoA: {"code":"FORBIDDEN","message":"No active membership in this organization."}`, final state `REVOKED`/`ACTIVE`. `SessionGuard` passes, then `OrganizationAccessGuard` re-reads a membership the winning request has just committed as `REVOKED`, and the loser gets `403` rather than the `409`/`401` the `losses` filter accepts. **R-007's actual invariant — exactly one success, the organization never reaches zero owners — held in all four.**

**How the error was made, which matters more than the error.** R-008's own occurrence-4 addendum had already established that both assertions emit the byte-identical `expected [] to have a length of 1 but got +0`, because both compare an empty array to a length of 1 and Vitest's default message names no variable. Having established it, nobody re-checked occurrences 1–3 against it. The original reading cited "line 451" where the log said 452, and every later investigation inherited that one-line error instead of re-deriving it. **It also explains D-6's 165 clean attempts completely: they were hunting a world in which neither request returned 200, and no such world ever occurred.** D-6's inference that its local repro "directly confirmed ... which only occurs when NEITHER status equals 200" is withdrawn on the same ground — the message it reproduced is ambiguous, so reproducing it confirmed nothing about which assertion CI hit. **D-6 stays CLOSED and its section is untouched**; what is withdrawn is one inference recorded in R-008, not D-6's own closure.

**The environmental differential was computed anyway, and it independently refutes F-3.** Two tooling scripts were added — `tools/diagnostics/environment-report.ts` and `tools/diagnostics/pg-activity-sample.ts` — and run against the full local suite. **This is the first measurement this repository has ever taken of how close the suite comes to `max_connections`**, a setting that previously appeared nowhere in the repository at all.

- Local: 8 cores → **7 Vitest workers** (`max(availableParallelism - 1, 1)`, `createForksPool`, vitest 2.1.9, non-watch). PostgreSQL 17.5, `max_connections` **100**, `superuser_reserved_connections` 3 → **97 usable**.

Wait events seen: `Client:ClientRead`, `IO:WalWrite`, `Lock:transactionid` — the last being exactly the row-lock contention `lockActiveForUpdate` exists to produce.

## 2026-09-02 — R-008 investigated properly: deadlock hypothesis REFUTED, root cause remains UNDETERMINED, not fixed

**Context:** `PHASE_1_DEBT_CLOSURE.md` D-6. R-008's test failed a third time during the D-4 slice, on a commit that changed zero production code — three occurrences total, none explained. `RISK_REGISTER.md` R-008 named `ORDER BY id` on `lockActiveForUpdate` as a candidate fix; the explicit trap this entry avoids is applying it and declaring victory because the flake didn't recur — that would prove nothing, since "fixed" and "didn't happen this time" are indistinguishable without a reproduction to test against.

**Step 1 — evidence already available, read in full for all three occurrences (CI runs `32987662296`, `33080832110` attempt 1, `33081324846` attempt 1):**

All three fail at the identical assertion (`expect(successes).toHaveLength(1)`, line 451 at the time) with the identical message, byte-for-byte: `AssertionError: expected [] to have a length of 1 but got +0`. **This message is unambiguous about one fact**, confirmed by deliberately reproducing the exact message locally with a throwaway test (`statuses = [403, 403]` → `successes = []` → identical `"expected [] to have a length of 1 but got +0"`): `successes.length` was genuinely `0` in every occurrence, meaning **neither** `resAtoB.status` nor `resBtoA.status` equaled the number `200`. This is stated as fact, not inference, because it is exactly what causes this exact message and no other status-length combination does.

Each occurrence's structured request log was searched for the failing test's own organization id specifically (each test run creates a fresh, randomly-suffixed org that appears nowhere else in a 800-1200 line log) — occurrence 1's org (`dcaf22c7-...`) and occurrence 3's org (`d29a26b8-...`) each appear in the log **exactly twice**, both as `revoke` calls, confirming both log lines belong to this test's own two concurrent requests and nothing else. **What those log lines show, taken at face value, is a `200` and a `403`** for each occurrence — which is arithmetically inconsistent with the test's own confirmed `successes.length === 0`, since a genuine `200` would make `successes.length` at least `1`. **This contradiction between the structured request log (one clean 200) and the test's own JavaScript computation (no 200 at all) is the real finding of this investigation, and it is not resolved.** Either the request log's `200` does not mean what it appears to mean under whatever produced these specific failures, or something about how `resAtoB.status`/`resBtoA.status` were read diverges from what the server actually sent — no evidence gathered here distinguishes between those explanations. Stated as an open contradiction, not papered over with the more comfortable of the two readings.

**What all three occurrences have in common:** identical assertion, identical message, no 5xx status anywhere in either request of any occurrence, no server-side exception log near any of the three (checked directly — the one `HttpExceptionFilter` "Unmapped HttpException status 418" / "undefined" log line near occurrence 1 was already identified in `RISK_REGISTER.md` R-008's own prior correction as belonging to `error-contract.integration.spec.ts`'s unrelated deliberate-leak test, timestamped ~16 seconds later; re-confirmed absent near occurrences 2 and 3 as well). **What differs:** nothing conclusive — the durations (34ms/51ms/66ms) and org ids vary as expected across independent runs, but no pattern beyond "the same assertion fails the same way" emerges from three data points. Three is enough to see that the failure mode is consistent, not enough to see a trigger.

**Step 2 — deliberate local reproduction, against real PostgreSQL:**

Two independent attempts, both against this machine's real PostgreSQL 17, both temporary and not committed:
1. A standalone script firing the exact two-concurrent-owners scenario (same helpers, same `Promise.all` shape as the real test) in a tight loop, **150 iterations**. Result: **0 anomalies**. Every iteration produced exactly `[200, 409]`, the documented happy-path outcome. `successes.length` was `1` all 150 times.
2. The **full local test suite** (`npx vitest run`, all 37 files, matching CI's actual concurrent-file execution rather than an isolated script) run **15 times**. Result: **380/380 tests passed on all 15 runs** — the two-concurrent-owners test never failed.

**165 total executions of this exact race, zero reproductions.** This is reported plainly rather than downplayed: it means the trigger condition, whatever it is, either does not exist on this machine's environment (Windows, native PostgreSQL 17, different CPU/IO scheduling than an Ubuntu GitHub Actions runner) or requires a load/timing condition neither a tight loop nor 15 sequential full-suite runs produced. No failure state was ever captured locally, so step 2's "capture the full failure state including final `memberships` rows" could not be performed — there was nothing to capture.

**Step 3 — the deadlock hypothesis, tested directly rather than inferred:**

- `pg_stat_database.deadlocks` was read before and after the 150-iteration reproduction script: **delta of 0**. Zero deadlocks recorded across 150 genuine concurrent-transaction races against real Postgres.
- All three real CI occurrences were re-checked specifically for a 5xx status on either request: **none found**. A Postgres deadlock (40P01) reaching the application unhandled would surface as an `HttpExceptionFilter`-logged unhandled exception and a 500 response — neither request in any of the three occurrences shows this.
- Postgres server-side logs from the actual failing CI runs could not be retrieved (GitHub Actions' ephemeral Postgres container is torn down at job end; no log artifact was ever configured to capture it) — this is a real limitation of this investigation, stated rather than hidden. The evidence against a deadlock is therefore drawn from (a) the local reproduction's own real Postgres, under the identical query pattern, and (b) the complete absence of any deadlock-shaped symptom (a 500) in any of the three real failures' own request/response evidence — not from having inspected the CI database's own logs directly.

**Verdict: the deadlock hypothesis is REFUTED by this evidence, not merely left unconfirmed.** `RISK_REGISTER.md` R-008 previously called it "structurally plausible... stated explicitly as a hypothesis, not a diagnosis" — that hedge was correct discipline at the time, and this step is what that discipline was for: the hypothesis was tested, and it does not hold up. Treating `lockActiveForUpdate`'s missing `ORDER BY` as the cause of these three failures would be treating the wrong thing.

**Step 4/5 — no fix applied, and why that is the honest outcome, not a shortfall:**

Per the task's own explicit instruction: a fix with no before/after measurement against a reproduction is not proven, it is hoped for. This investigation produced **no reproduction to measure against** — 165 executions, zero anomalies. Applying `ORDER BY id` to `lockActiveForUpdate` anyway, "just in case," was considered and rejected: the evidence gathered here does not point at lock ordering at all (no deadlock symptom, ever) — it points at an unresolved contradiction between server-logged and test-computed status values, a different layer of the system entirely. Applying an unmotivated defensive change here would not fix anything the evidence identifies, and would risk being mistaken later for "R-008 was addressed" when it was not. `ORDER BY id` remains named as a candidate in `RISK_REGISTER.md`, unapplied, for the same reason it always was: standard defensive practice for `SELECT ... FOR UPDATE` over an unordered set, independent of whether it is this bug's cause — which the evidence here says it probably is not.

**What WAS done, deliberately, regardless of root cause:** the test's own diagnostics. Per this task's own "ALSO CONSIDER": **yes, the final `memberships` state should be captured independent of the status-code assertion, and now is** (`apps/api/membership-revoke.integration.spec.ts`) — the DB read that used to happen only after `successes`/`losses` passed now happens unconditionally, right after the two requests resolve, before any assertion that could throw. When the outcome does not match the expected shape, a full diagnostic dump (raw statuses, their `typeof`, both response bodies and headers, both final membership statuses, the org and membership ids) is logged to `console.error` so it appears in CI output. **Justification:** every one of the three real failures produced almost no diagnostic information, which is exactly why this investigation needed 165 local executions instead of reading the third occurrence's own log — fixing that gap is valuable independent of whether the underlying cause is ever found, and costs nothing on the passing path (it changes when data is read, not what the test asserts or how it can pass or fail). This is a test-observability improvement, not a change to `membership.revoke`'s business rules — nothing about which requests succeed, which fail, or what the invariant is was touched.

**Step 6 — R-008 re-rated:**

Likelihood changed from "Low — reproduced exactly once" to **Medium**. Basis, stated as a rough estimate from a small sample, not a rigorously derived rate: 3 occurrences across roughly 20 distinct CI runs observed across this project's history (~15% empirical rate), consistent with 0 occurrences across 15 local full-suite runs (statistically unsurprising at a true rate anywhere up to roughly 18-20% with only 15 trials). "Low, reproduced exactly once" undersold what is now a repeatedly-observed pattern; "Medium" reflects that without overclaiming precision the sample size cannot support. Impact reassessed alongside: the deadlock-shaped "unhandled 500 masking a real commit" impact story is **withdrawn** (refuted, not just unconfirmed) — what remains is that this is the regression guard for R-007, a critical data-integrity invariant, and a test that fails for an unexplained reason erodes trust in that guard specifically at the moment Phase 2 adds materially more concurrent-writer surface (payment callbacks, subscription state transitions, reconciliation sweeps) that will need the same kind of guard trusted without reservation. Impact stays **Medium**, now grounded in "this is the regression guard for a critical invariant, not proven exempt from the thing it's supposed to catch" rather than in a specific (and now refuted) mechanism.

**Status: R-008 stays OPEN.** Not closed, not "probably fine" — genuinely unresolved, with a lowered-but-non-zero uncertainty about whether R-007's actual invariant has ever been at risk in these three occurrences (every reading of the available evidence, even the unresolved one, is consistent with at most one of the two concurrent revokes having committed each time — never both — so the zero-owner dead end itself has no evidence of ever occurring; what's undetermined is why the test's own accounting disagrees with the server log, not whether the invariant held). `PHASE_1_DEBT_CLOSURE.md` D-6 is closed on a different basis than "problem solved": the debt was that this had never been investigated as pre-Phase-2 work, under the required rigor (reproduce, test the leading hypothesis directly, only fix what evidence supports); that investigation has now happened, and its honest, negative-for-the-easy-answer conclusion is the deliverable.

## 2026-09-01 — Closing Phase 1 debt D-4: ESLint + typescript-eslint + Prettier, not Biome

**Context:** `06_IMPLEMENTATION_PLAN.md` Phase 0 item 2 requires both a linter and a formatter in the toolchain inventory — `PHASE_1_DEBT_CLOSURE.md` D-4, the last unmet Phase 0 deliverable, unbuilt since Phase 0 itself and never caught by any of the three Phase 1 gate reviews (all three audited Phase 1's own exit criteria, never Phase 0's checklist).

**Options considered:**
  A. Biome — a single Rust binary covering both linting and formatting, near-instant, minimal config, one dependency instead of several.
  B. ESLint + `typescript-eslint` + Prettier — the incumbent, heavier setup: more devDependencies, slower than Biome at any codebase size, but `typescript-eslint` can run **type-aware** rules (rules that consult the actual TypeScript type checker, not just syntax) where Biome's linter is syntax-only and cannot.

**Decision: B.** The type-aware distinction is the deciding factor, not a generic preference — argued from this codebase's own history, not from linter benchmarks in the abstract. `tsconfig.json` is `strict` with `noUncheckedIndexedAccess`, meaning this project already leans on the type checker for correctness beyond what most codebases ask of it; type-aware lint rules (`@typescript-eslint/no-floating-promises`, `no-misused-promises`, `no-unnecessary-condition`, `require-await`, `no-confusing-void-expression`) target exactly the class of bug an async, Promise-heavy, Kysely-and-NestJS codebase like this one actually produces — every controller in this repository is `async`, every repository call returns a `Promise`, and a missed `await` on `recordAuditEventDurable` (or its replacement, `runCapabilityAttempt`) is precisely a `no-floating-promises` finding, not a syntax-level one Biome could catch. This is not a hypothetical class of bug for this specific codebase: `sessions.revoked_at`'s "silently un-updatable through Kysely" typing defect (found and fixed twice, independently, first for `sessions.revoked_at` then again for `memberships.updated_at` — `CLAUDE.md`'s own history) is exactly the shape of subtle async/type-interaction bug a type-aware linter exists to catch before it ships, not after an integration test happens to notice.

Biome's real advantages — speed, one dependency, less config surface — are weighed and don't outweigh this at this codebase's current size: `typescript-eslint`'s type-aware overhead scales with project size, and this project (a few dozen source files as of Phase 1's close) will run it in low single-digit seconds, nowhere near threatening ADR-030's "harness runs in under two minutes" standard this project already holds itself to for the conformance harness. The dependency-count argument, which correctly stopped a runtime dependency for `modules/money`/`modules/calendar` (`AGENTS.md` §4, "prefer zero new runtime dependencies if the standard library genuinely suffices"), applies with less force here: ESLint/Prettier are **devDependencies**, never shipped, never a runtime attack surface or a bundle-size cost — the reasoning that correctly kept `modules/calendar` dependency-free doesn't transfer to dev tooling with the same weight.

**Package choices:** `eslint` (flat config, `eslint.config.js` — ESLint 9+'s config format, matching this project's ESM-first conventions), `typescript-eslint` (the unified package bundling parser + plugin + type-aware configs, not the older split `@typescript-eslint/parser` + `@typescript-eslint/eslint-plugin` packages), `eslint-config-prettier` (turns off ESLint's own stylistic rules so Prettier is the only source of formatting opinions — no fighting between the two), `prettier`.

**Prettier config, decided rather than left at defaults:** `printWidth: 120` (Prettier's default 80 would have wrapped far more of this codebase's already-consistent, fairly verbose-identifier style than the code as written actually needed — checked directly: most existing files already matched Prettier's defaults byte-for-byte once CRLF/LF was accounted for, and 120 kept the genuine reformatting diff to 61 files instead of a much larger one); `semi`/`singleQuote`/`trailingComma`/`tabWidth` left at what the codebase already did (`true`/`false`/`"all"`/`2`) rather than picked independently, since the existing style already matched; `endOfLine: "lf"`, matching `.gitattributes`' existing `eol=lf` normalization exactly rather than leaving it to whatever a given checkout's `core.autocrlf` produces. `.prettierignore` excludes `*.md` **entirely**, not case by case: `06`'s own item 2 groups "linter, formatter" with the TypeScript/NestJS toolchain, not documentation, and this repository's dated records (`DECISION_LOG.md`, `RISK_REGISTER.md`, every `PHASE_1_GATE_*.md`) must never be rewritten — excluding the whole extension removes that risk structurally rather than trusting a maintained exception list to stay complete as new dated records are added. Also excludes `openapi.json`/`PROJECT_GRAPH.md`/`tools/graph/project-graph.json` (each has its own generator and its own drift check) and `tools/conformance/fixtures/**` (deliberately-invalid-by-design, same reasoning `tsconfig.json` already excludes them for).

**D-4: rules turned off, decided per rule, not blanket-suppressed.** Ran `npx eslint .` cold against the full tree: 326 findings across ~9 distinct rules. Read the actual file distribution for every rule that fired more than a handful of times before deciding anything — per-rule:

- **`@typescript-eslint/no-unsafe-member-access`/`no-unsafe-argument`/`no-unsafe-assignment`/`no-unsafe-call`/`no-unsafe-return`** (207 findings combined) — **205 of 207 in `apps/api/*.integration.spec.ts`**, checked file-by-file, not estimated. Root cause: `supertest`'s `response.body` (a parsed HTTP JSON response) and its `app`/`getHttpServer()` argument types are untyped by the library's own design — every `expect(response.body.code).toBe(...)`-shaped assertion trips `no-unsafe-member-access` once per property accessed. Fixing this at every call site would mean adding explicit response-shape types (or casts) across essentially every integration test in the repository — exactly the mass refactor `AGENTS.md` §4 forbids for a tooling slice, for findings that fail the *test itself* if wrong, not a real user in production. **Decision: turned off for `**/*.spec.ts` only**, via a `files: ["**/*.spec.ts"]` override in `eslint.config.js` — production code keeps the full rule set.
